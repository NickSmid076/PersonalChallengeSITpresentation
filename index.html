02

Patience

Answer every question calmly, also the third time, and never take over the mouse. Measured by the participants, the caretaker and my own notes.

My core values are perseverance, being goal-oriented and fulfilment. My pitfall, from my personal handbook, is that I go from thinking straight to acting and can be too direct. So goal one: explain simply, with an average satisfaction of at least 7.5. Goal two: patience, even the third time someone asks.

Element 3 · Bias and identityNick Smid ·

My assumptions were the problem

They don't care about technology→They cared. They were scared.
It's too complex for them→They learn fast with a patient teacher.
Silence means they aren't paying attention→Silence was fear: of a wrong answer, or of me.

Why: I'm 23 and work with AI every day, so I forgot what not knowing feels like: the curse of knowledge (Camerer et al., 1989). Goal-oriented and Dutch-direct, I acted on my first impression: fast System 1 thinking (Kahneman, 2011).

One bias that influenced my actions was that I thought elderly people didn't care about technology and couldn't really learn it. The impact was that I explained too much, too fast and too directly, so people closed off. What I learned is that they did care, and that the teacher makes the difference.
Element 3 · Adaptability logNick Smid ·

Three moments that changed how I teach

1

Nobody answered

Situation
I asked again and called people by name. Friction.
Adaptation
Stopped pushing. Played games with them before session 2 and sat between them.
Outcome
Questions came on their own.
2

Repeating myself

Situation
The same step, again and again. Their attention dropped, and so did my patience.
Adaptation
A break: coffee, sweets, a funny video. Then back to the step.
Outcome
Breaks became a fixed part.
3

Fixing it alone

Situation
I worked harder on my slides and expected more from the group.
Adaptation
Asked friends with a social work degree: “the training is secondary.”
Outcome
Sessions 3 and 4 were the most engaged.
Three moments forced me to adapt. When nobody answered, I stopped pushing and sat between them instead of in front. When I kept repeating myself, I learned to take a break with coffee. And when I tried to fix it alone, which is my usual pattern, I finally asked for help.

This challenge could not have been done from my own perspective alone. I saw a knowledge gap. Social workers, the care staff and the elderly themselves showed me it was a trust and comfort gap. That changed my approach from explaining as much as possible to explaining less, slower and with breaks.

Element 2 · SustainabilityNick Smid ·

It's not my workshop anymore

Already arranged

  • PowerPoint handed over to the organisation
  • Their IT person gives it once a month
  • Mooi Werk keeps inviting elderly from the area

What could break it

  • The IT person explains it too technically, just like I did
  • Scams change: phone fraud doubled in 2024
  • Attendance drops when the novelty is gone

How to make it last

  • Keep the coffee moment and the same room
  • A note on how to explain, not only what
  • Update fake email examples; train a second trainer

People didn't come back for the content. They came back because it felt safe and social.

The workshop now belongs to the organisation: I handed over the PowerPoint and their IT person will give it every month. The biggest risk is that he explains it too technically, exactly my own mistake. So I'd keep the social part that made people come back and train a second trainer at Mooi Werk.
